Economic Model — v0.5

Emission anchored to bandwidth, not time

Interactive simulation of the eWatts emission formula. Supply expands when the network grows, contracts when it shrinks, and stabilizes when it plateaus — all from a single ratio: effective bandwidth over historical average.

Every eWatt is backed by energy spent on DAG-bound computation. The protocol does not target a price or an inflation rate. Instead, it adjusts emission proportionally to how committed bandwidth changes relative to its trailing average. There is no oracle, no governance parameter, and no pre-programmed decay.

How the formula works

Emission per block follows a simple ratio:

emission = clamp(100 × total_eff / hist_avg, 5, 2,000)

total_eff is the sum of all miners' effective committed bandwidth (GB/s) declared in the current block. hist_avg is the trailing average of total_eff over the last 100 blocks (DIFFICULTY_WINDOW_BLOCKS).

When bandwidth is exactly at its trailing average, emission stays at the base rate of 100 Ewatt per block. If bandwidth doubles, emission doubles (up to the ceiling of 2,000). If bandwidth halves, emission halves (down to the floor of 5). The system self-adjusts to whatever pace the network actually grows at.

The first 10,000 blocks have a ramp-up cap of 80%: no single miner can earn more than 80% of the block reward, even if they dominate the network. Any excess is burned. This prevents a single early miner from capturing the entire emission during bootstrap.

Miner outputs from the first 10,000 blocks are locked for 50,000 blocks (about 347 days after the last block in the bootstrap window). This ensures early miners have a long-term commitment to the network's health, not a short-term extractive window.

The Value Reference (VR)

The code computes a Value Reference defined as:

VR = total_kwh / total_ewatts_mined

Total kWh is derived from the network's effective bandwidth over time, multiplied by the physical energy cost per DAG access (J_PER_ACCESS = 3.75 µJ). This gives a purely computational lower bound on the energy required to produce each eWatt. No oracle, no quotation service, no subjective input. The market price of eWatt will float above this floor by whatever premium the market assigns to a neutral settlement layer.

What the protocol does not do

  • No price target. There is no $1.00/eWatt or any other programmed price. The market discovers price; the protocol only tracks energy cost.
  • No inflation target. No arbitrary inflation rate codified. Supply adjusts to bandwidth, period.
  • No supply cap. Hard caps create scarcity pressure that conflicts with settlement function.
  • No halving schedule. No time-based reward decay. Emission responds to network activity, not a calendar.
  • No oracles. No external price feeds, no off-chain dependencies, no governance committees.
  • No founder allocation. No pre-mine, no admin keys, no privileged minting.

Everything emerges from two numbers per block (total_eff and hist_avg) and a handful of physical constants that never change.

Open questions, acknowledged

Long-term security budget. Once network bandwidth stabilizes, per-block rewards are proportional to that stable bandwidth. Transaction fees will eventually dominate miner compensation, as they do on Bitcoin. Whether fees will be sufficient is an open question that cannot be predicted in advance.

Adoption is uncertain. Every forward-looking value in this dashboard depends on bandwidth growth that has not happened. The protocol exists. The economic model exists. Adoption is a bet, not a derivation.

DRAM Latency & Energy Calibration

[1] O. Mutlu and L. Subramanian, "Understanding and Improving the Latency of DRAM-Based Memory Systems," Communications of the ACM, vol. 64, no. 8, pp. 80–89, 2021. — DRAM latency improved only ~1.3x in ~20 years while capacity grew 128x and bandwidth ~20x. [PDF]

[2] Y. Lee, J. Kim, and O. Mutlu, "Tiered-Latency DRAM (TL-DRAM): A Low-Latency DRAM Architecture," HPCA, 2013. — Demonstrates that DRAM row activation latency has remained fundamentally limited. [PDF]

[3] DRAM Scaling Graph (1999–2017). — Capacity up ~128x, bandwidth ~20x, latency essentially flat. [ResearchGate]

[4] CHARM: A High-Performance Cache-Coherent Architecture for Heterogeneous Systems, ISCA, 2013. — Related work on DRAM latency limitations. [PDF]

[5] T. Vogelsang, "Understanding the Energy Consumption of Dynamic Random Access Memories," MICRO, 2010. — Most-cited paper on DRAM energy per bit across generations. Energy per access improved significantly to ~2010, then decelerated.

[6] O. Mutlu et al., ISCA / HPCA proceedings (multiple years). — While DRAM bandwidth exploded, energy per random access improved modestly. Row activation energy remains the dominant term.

[7] W. A. Wulf and S. A. McKee, "Hitting the Memory Wall: Implications of the Obvious," ACM SIGARCH Computer Architecture News, vol. 23, no. 1, pp. 20–24, 1995. — The original "memory wall" paper that predicted the divergence between processor speed and memory latency.
