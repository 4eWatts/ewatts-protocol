Economic Model — v0.5

Free entry as equilibrium

Every node running eWatts is a continuous bid in an implicit auction. The block reward is fixed at 100 eWatts. Free entry and exit mean the network finds its own size: the margin goes to zero, and bandwidth becomes a function of price and electricity cost, not an exogenous assumption.

The key result: Under free entry, the network's Value Reference converges to P / pelec — the market price of eWatt divided by the price of electricity. Hardware efficiency drops out of the equilibrium entirely. A 5% efficiency improvement does not weaken the anchor; it attracts 5% more nodes. Total network energy and the VR stay constant.

Equilibrium nodes vs market price

At any price P, free entry drives the node count to N = 8,000 × P / pelec. Linear: if P doubles, N doubles.

Value Reference = P / pelec

The VR converges to market price divided by electricity cost. It is not a fixed number of kWh. Efficiency does not appear in the equation.

Network energy over 20 years

Efficiency improves 5%/yr (compounding). Each year, node power drops, but that opens margin, new nodes enter, and network energy stays flat. No drift.

Nodes absorbing efficiency gains

As node power drops from 75 W toward ~25 W over 20 years, the equilibrium node count rises to keep total cost = total reward. VR stays at P / pelec.

eWatt unit economics (per node)

Revenue per node = 100/N × P. Cost per node = watts × 600s × pelec / 3.6e6. Margin goes to zero in equilibrium. Entry closes any gap.

Bitcoin parallel: the lag advantage

Bitcoin has the same production-cost-revenue identity, but ASIC replacement takes months. eWatts hardware is commodity DRAM: entry and exit happen in minutes.

The derivation

Each node running eWatts consumes W watts of power. Over one 600-second block, the energy cost is:

c = W × 600 × pelec / 3,600,000  ($/block/node)

There are N identical nodes. The block reward is 100 eWatts. With free entry, competition drives the margin to zero. Total network cost equals total reward value:

N × c = 100 × P
∴ N = 100 × P / c = 8,000 × P / pelec

Network energy per block:

E = N × W × 600 / 3,600,000  (kWh/block)
∴ E = 100 × P / pelec

Dividing by the block reward (100 eWatts) gives the marginal Value Reference at equilibrium:

VR = E / 100 = P / pelec

Watts per node cancel out. Hardware efficiency does not appear in the equilibrium VR. The anchor follows price and electricity cost, not the generation of DRAM.

What efficiency drift actually does

When hardware efficiency improves (e.g., DDR5 replaces DDR4), node power W drops while maintaining the same ~1 GB/s bandwidth. This cuts the per-node cost c. Margin opens, and new nodes enter. N rises exactly enough to close the margin. Total network energy and the VR return to exactly where they were.

The drift is absorbed entirely by network size, not by the anchor. The only scenario where efficiency drift matters for the VR is the one where node entry is frozen by assumption — which is the scenario without the market mechanism that exists.

This is the same identity that governs Bitcoin (Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance estimates Bitcoin’s energy consumption using the same production-cost-equals-revenue identity). The difference is speed of adjustment: Bitcoin requires ASIC manufacturing lead times and months of mining profitability lag. eWatts runs on commodity DRAM that billions of devices already have, and entry/exit happens in minutes.

Why a burn auction is unnecessary

An explicit bidding mechanism (miners burning eWatt for additional reward weight) would also stabilize the system. But compare where the margin goes:

  • Free entry: Margin absorbed by new nodes. More participants, more distribution, wider decentralization. Hardware anyone already owns.
  • Burn auction: Margin absorbed by supply destruction. Same incumbents keep mining. Burn creates a barrier: you need eWatt to bid, which injects a proof-of-stake component.

For a protocol built on accessibility and neutrality, absorbing margin via entry is strictly superior to absorbing it via burn. The leilão Gustavo asked about already exists. It is the free entry of commodity hardware nodes.

Bottom line. The protocol does not need a new mechanism. It needs the testnet to demonstrate that entry responds to margin. That is the hypothesis the testnet validates or refutes.

The real risk: lag in an illiquid network

The equilibrium assumes instant entry and exit. In practice, during the bootstrap phase with few nodes and low liquidity, the response to a margin signal has lag. During that lag, efficiency drift affects real returns.

This risk is real, measurable, and temporary. It exists on every young PoW network. eWatts minimizes it relative to other PoW networks because no ASIC fabrication lag exists, but it does not eliminate it entirely. The bootstrap phase is when monitoring matters most.

What the protocol does not do

  • No price target. No $1.00/eWatt programmed value. P is a market output.
  • No inflation target. Supply adjusts to bandwidth, period.
  • No supply cap. No programmed scarcity. Always energy-backed.
  • No halving schedule. No time-based decay. Emission responds to network activity.
  • No oracles. No price feeds, no external dependencies, no governance committees.
  • No burn auction. Free entry is the auction. No mechanism needed beyond what already exists.

Open questions, updated

Does entry respond to margin? The equilibrium derivation assumes it does. Testnet data on node churn in response to profitability is the evidence that confirms or contradicts the model.

Long-term security budget. Once network bandwidth stabilizes, per-block rewards are proportional to stable bandwidth. Transaction fees will eventually dominate miner compensation. Whether fees suffice is an open question that cannot be predicted in advance.
